# Findings v0

This is the first full run of the atlas pipeline (phases 1 to 4), done overnight on 2026-11-19. It
lists every candidate that passed the statistical gate and was reviewed, with its evidence and the
reviewer's reason. The break tables come after the candidates. The document is generated by
`python -m src.atlas.report`. Only this introduction is written by hand.

## What came out

Pairs found inside the theme slice gave no finding. Pairs across the whole catalog gave two stable ones, and neither is the kind people would share.

- **Theme slice (housing plus cost of living).** 206 tables became 6,801 series. Under the
  first version of the rules, 15 candidates passed the gate. The reviewer dropped all 15, and each
  drop names a concrete reason:
  - a segment counted inside its own total;
  - a 2026 price spike that made a weak link look strong;
  - the USD rate passing into SGD-denominated prices;
  - prices and rents built from the same market.

  The tightened rules (below) leave 3 candidates, and the reviewer dropped those as well. The
  15 first-rules drops and their rationales are archived in the appendix.
- **Whole catalog.** 1,053 tables became 48,637 series. Under the tightened rules, 120 candidates
  pass the gate. I ran the reviewer (Sonnet 5.5) three times over the top 60, each with an empty
  cache. Two pairs were kept in all three runs:
  - **Rainy months have cooler nights.** Monthly rain days against the mean daily minimum
    temperature. This is real but close to obvious.
  - **The 10-year government bond yield moves with import prices in the same month**, from 1998
    to 2026. An earlier Sonnet review of the theme slice dropped the same pair as a 2026 one-off.
    That review used an earlier prompt, which required a rationale but didn't yet list the gates
    the pair had passed (archived in the appendix). Treat it as kept but contested.

  One pair was kept in only one run of three: a weaker Singapore dollar against higher food prices
  in the same month. It is not counted. Below, the "Kept findings" section of the whole-catalog
  store shows the first of the three runs. Its drop table breaks down as follows:
  - 21 are a part against its whole. About half are job-vacancy and retrenchment tables, which
    publish one quantity several ways. The rest are trade, visitor arrivals, vessel arrivals and
    vehicle counts;
  - 15 are obvious;
  - 11 are artefacts;
  - 5 are the same quantity;
  - 5 are identities.

  So two findings survived. Neither is the kind people would share.
- **Breaks are the stronger material.** 133 series show a structural break above the 1% critical
  value. In the theme slice, the strongest line up with known events: the 1997 peak in the resale index, the
  2012 to 2013 cooling measures, and the 2018 vehicle-growth change. The notes are labelled
  guesses unless an event is clearly identified.

## Why so few

- **Power.** Most series are quarterly or annual. Pairs tested at the median have about 22
  effective test points. A relationship has to reach |r| ≈ 0.65 to 0.67 to clear the multiple-testing
  bar across the catalog. In the housing slice the bar is 0.71 to 0.77, and BH kills 5,440 of the
  5,461 pairs tested. Annual series are not paired at all.
- **One quantity, many tables.** data.gov.sg publishes the same quantity in many cuts (by industry,
  by occupation, monthly and quarterly, at current and constant prices). Rule 2 collapses the
  obvious copies. The rest, such as a component inside a total in another table, need the LLM
  reviewer, which is doing that job well.
- **The calendar.** Monthly totals share calendar effects that subtracting each month's mean
  doesn't remove: February's length, and Chinese New Year moving between January and February.
  One reviewed pair (F&B services against foreign aircraft movements) was linked only by
  this. Three more drops cite it alongside other reasons.

## Changes made tonight because of what the data showed

- Phase 2 added a records reader. HDB resale, rental and CEA tables have one row per transaction,
  so they are now monthly medians and counts, overall and by town or flat type. Those tables were
  re-downloaded in full through the bulk endpoint. The cache had them capped at 10,000 rows. Asked for
  more, the paged API threw deep-page 404s, which `fetch.py` reads as "gone".
- The COVID gate excludes 2020 to 2022 and requires both the rank check and the ex-COVID check.
  Most COVID-recovery pairs had been passing before. One still gets through the gate: CEA
  rental transactions against aircraft arrivals, at a lag of 3 months. The reviewer drops it.
- New rules drop a pair whose test correlation is more than double its training correlation (one
  episode, not a stable link), and keep one candidate per pair of datasets.
- After this run, a verifier found the records detector treating a few per-category tables as event
  records, so the detector was tightened. A rebuild matches 1,052 tables rather than 1,053; the
  stores reported below predate the fix. The only affected tables are short annual and quarterly
  ones, which are never paired.
- The reviewer must give a rationale for every verdict, and the default model is now Sonnet 5.5.
  Haiku also dropped every theme candidate, but gave no reasons.

## What would get to 20 findings (for Anselm to choose)

1. **Lead with breaks and events, not pairs.** Breaks shared by many series at the same date make
   strong stories ("what changed in 2013"), and they need no pair power.
2. **Use within-table structure for the housing theme.** Resale medians by month for each town and each flat
   type (119 months, 2017 to 2026) support panel questions with real power: which towns lead
   the others, and where prices diverged.
3. **Add a calendar adjustment for monthly flows** (trading days, Chinese New Year). Then rerun
   the cross-catalog pairs. That should lift monthly power and remove a class of false links.
4. **Require reviewer agreement.** Verdicts changed between runs and between prompt versions. A
   finding should need a keep in several runs, or from two models.
5. **Relax the bar to show "suggestive" links with the label "suggestive".** That conflicts with the
   honesty bar you picked, so it's your call, and I haven't done it.

## Store `atlas/all`

1053 of 1855 tables became 48,637 series. The statistical gate passed 120 candidates; the top 60 were reviewed and 3 kept. 133 series have a break above the 1% sup-F critical value.

### Kept findings

**In Singapore, months with more rainy days tend to have cooler nights.**

- x: Rainfall - Monthly Number of Rain Days
- y: Surface Air Temperature - Monthly Mean Daily Minimum
- evidence: same period; r train -0.52, r test -0.53 (n 160), ex-COVID -0.48, BH p 4.8e-05; span 1982-02..2026-07
- explanation: From 1982 to 2026, when a month had more rain days than usual for the time of year, its average overnight low tended to be cooler, and vice versa. The two move together in the same month (lag 0). This is an association in month-to-month changes, not proof that rain alone cools the nights.
- caveat: Association only: cloud cover, wind and monsoon conditions could drive both rain days and night temperatures.
- caveat: The relationship is moderate, not tight; many months will not follow the pattern.
- caveat: Rain days count how often it rained, not how much, so heavy and light rain are treated the same.
- caveat: Both series are weather observations and may come from the same station network, so they may not be fully independent.
- idea (verified): Rain days vs night-time temperature tracker. A monthly chart showing each month's change in rain days next to the change in average daily minimum temperature, highlighting months that break the usual pattern.
- idea (verified): Nowcasting night temperatures from rain counts. Use the number of rain days so far in a month as a rough early indicator of whether that month's minimum temperature will come in below or above its seasonal norm.
- idea (brainstormed): Warm-night comfort and energy demand. Explore whether months with fewer rain days and warmer nights coincide with higher household electricity use for cooling. This goes beyond what was tested here.
- reviewer: These are two different weather measures (a count of rain days and an average night-time temperature), so neither contains the other and no identity links them. The negative link is stable between train (-0.5189) and test (-0.5282), holds by rank and without 2020-2022, and is less obvious for night minimums than for daytime highs.

**Singapore's 10-year government bond yield tends to rise in the same months that import prices rise, and fall when they fall.**

- x: Current Banks Interest Rates (End Of Period), Monthly: Government Securities - 10-Year Bond Yield
- y: Import Price Index, By Commodity Section (1-Digit Level), Base Year 2023 = 100, Monthly: Import Price Index - Overall Items
- evidence: same period; r train +0.28, r test +0.45 (n 101), ex-COVID +0.46, BH p 0.004; span 1998-07..2026-09
- explanation: Month to month, changes in the 10-year government bond yield tend to move in the same direction as changes in the import price index, with no lag (same month). This was observed over 1998-07 to 2026-09 and was stronger in the later part of the data than the earlier part. It is an association only and does not show that one drives the other.
- caveat: The two series move in the same month, so neither can be shown to lead the other.
- caveat: The link is moderate, not tight, and most month-to-month movement in each series is unrelated to the other.
- caveat: The recent excerpt includes a very large one-month jump in the import price index (2026-03), which coincides with a rise in the yield. The later period may be influenced by such episodes.
- caveat: The import price index is rebased to 2023 = 100, and its composition, such as fuel, may be a hidden common driver, for example through inflation expectations.
- caveat: The bond yield is an end-of-period reading and the import price index is a monthly figure, so their timing may not line up exactly.
- idea (verified): Yield vs import prices tracker. A simple chart that shows monthly changes in the 10-year yield next to changes in import prices, and highlights the months when they moved the same way.
- idea (verified): Same-month sanity check for import price shocks. When the import price index jumps, check whether the bond yield moved in the same month, and use the pair as a rough cross-check of market reaction to price news.
- idea (brainstormed): Which import categories matter most. Break the import price index into its commodity sections to see whether the link with bond yields comes mainly from one section such as fuels. This goes beyond what was tested.
- reviewer: These are two different quantities from different sources: a financial market yield and a trade price index. There is no identity or component link. The relationship is positive in both the training period (0.28) and the test period (0.45), and it holds by rank (0.42) and without 2020-2022 (0.46), so it is not just one outlier.

**When the Singapore dollar slides against the US dollar, food prices in Singapore tend to rise in the same month.**

- x: Domestic Supply Price Index, By Commodity Section (1-Digit Level), Base Year 2023 = 100, Monthly: Food & Live Animals
- y: Exchange Rates (Average For Period), Monthly: US Dollar
- evidence: same period; r train +0.22, r test +0.43 (n 139), ex-COVID +0.40, BH p 0.00021; span 1988-02..2026-09
- explanation: From 1988 to 2026, month-to-month changes in the Singapore dollar's rate against the US dollar tend to move with changes in the domestic supply price of food and live animals, with no lag (0 months). The link is moderate rather than tight (r about 0.43 on the later part of the data). It is associated with, not proof of, exchange-rate effects on food import costs.
- caveat: Moderate strength only: the exchange rate accounts for a small share of month-to-month food price changes.
- caveat: The relationship appears at lag 0, so it does not give advance warning of food price moves.
- caveat: The link was stronger in the later test period (0.4285) than in the earlier training period (0.2153), so it may not be equally strong in every era.
- caveat: The food index is rebased to 2023=100 and spans 1974 onward, so its history may be spliced across base years.
- caveat: Both series are likely to react to the same global conditions, such as commodity cycles and US dollar strength, so a third factor may drive both.
- idea (verified): Same-month food price vs exchange rate tracker. Plot monthly changes in the food supply price index next to changes in the Singapore dollar's rate against the US dollar, so readers can see how often they move in the same direction.
- idea (brainstormed): Exchange-rate sensitivity across commodity sections. Repeat the comparison across other commodity sections of the supply price index to see whether food is unusually sensitive to the exchange rate.
- idea (brainstormed): Currency-adjusted food price view. Build a view that separates the part of food price changes that coincides with currency moves from the remainder, to help readers read food inflation.
- reviewer: The two series come from different sources and are not linked by an identity or a component relationship. The tie between a weaker or stronger Singapore dollar and food supply prices is plausible but not trivial, and it holds ex-COVID (0.4033) and by rank (0.4079). The test correlation is higher than the train one (0.4285 vs 0.2153), but that looks like a stronger recent link rather than one episode, so I kept it with a caveat.
