# Findings v0

This is the first full run of the atlas pipeline (phases 1 to 4), done overnight on 2026-11-19. It
lists every candidate that passed the statistical gate and was reviewed, with its evidence and the
reviewer's reason. The break tables come after the candidates. The document is generated by
`python -m src.atlas.report`. Only this introduction is written by hand.

## What came out

Pairs found inside the theme slice gave no shareable finding. Pairs across the whole catalog gave one.

- **Theme slice (housing plus cost of living).** 206 tables became 6,801 series. Under the
  first version of the rules, 15 candidates passed the gate. The reviewer dropped all 15, and each
  drop names a concrete reason:
  - a segment counted inside its own total;
  - a 2026 price spike that made a weak link look strong;
  - the USD rate passing into SGD-denominated prices;
  - prices and rents built from the same market.

  The tightened rules (below) leave 3 candidates, and the reviewer dropped those as well.
- **Whole catalog.** 1,053 tables became 48,637 series, and 120 candidates passed the gate. The
  reviewer read the top 60 and kept 1: bond yields move with import prices in the same month.
  The drops break down as follows:
  - 21 are a part against its whole (job-vacancy and retrenchment tables publish one quantity
    several ways);
  - 17 are obvious (rain days against humidity);
  - 10 are artefacts;
  - 8 are the same quantity;
  - 3 are identities.
- **Breaks are the stronger material.** 133 series show a structural break above the 1% critical
  value. In the theme slice, the strongest line up with known events: the 1997 peak in the resale index, the
  2012 to 2013 cooling measures, and the 2018 vehicle-growth change. The notes are labelled
  guesses unless an event is clearly identified.

## Why so few

- **Power.** Most series are quarterly or annual. Pairs tested at the median have about 22
  effective test points. A relationship has to reach |r| ≈ 0.65 to clear the multiple-testing bar.
  Housing pairs mostly die at that step. Annual series are not paired at all.
- **One quantity, many tables.** data.gov.sg publishes the same quantity in many cuts (by industry,
  by occupation, monthly and quarterly, at current and constant prices). Rule 2 collapses the
  obvious copies. The rest, such as a component inside a total in another table, need the LLM
  reviewer, which is doing that job well.
- **The calendar.** Monthly totals share calendar effects that subtracting each month's mean
  doesn't remove: February's length, and Chinese New Year moving between January and February.
  The reviewer caught at least two pairs linked only by this.

## Changes made tonight because of what the data showed

- Phase 2 added a records reader. HDB resale, rental and CEA tables have one row per transaction,
  so they are now monthly medians and counts, overall and by town or flat type. Those tables were
  re-downloaded in full through the bulk endpoint. The paged API had capped them at 10,000 rows
  and then reported them as gone.
- The COVID gate excludes 2020 to 2022 and requires both the rank check and the ex-COVID check.
  COVID-recovery pairs, such as aircraft movements against property transactions, had been passing.
- New rules drop a pair whose test correlation is more than double its training correlation (one
  episode, not a stable link), and keep one candidate per pair of datasets.
- The reviewer must give a rationale for every verdict, and the default model is now Sonnet 5.5.
  Haiku also dropped every theme candidate, but gave no reasons.

## What would get to 20 findings (for Anselm to choose)

1. **Lead with breaks and events, not pairs.** Breaks shared by many series at the same date make
   strong stories ("what changed in 2013"), and they need no pair power.
2. **Use within-table structure for the housing theme.** Resale medians by month for each town and each flat
   type (119 months, 2017 to 2026) support panel questions with real power: which towns lead
   the others, and where prices diverged.
3. **Add a calendar adjustment for monthly flows** (trading days, Chinese New Year). Then rerun
   the cross-catalog pairs. That should lift monthly power and remove a class of false links.
4. **Relax the bar to show "suggestive" links with the label "suggestive".** That conflicts with the
   honesty bar you picked, so it's your call, and I haven't done it.

### Kept findings

**When the price of Singapore's imports jumps, the government's 10-year borrowing rate has tended to rise in the same month.**

- x: Current Banks Interest Rates (End Of Period), Monthly: Government Securities - 10-Year Bond Yield
- y: Import Price Index, By Commodity Section (1-Digit Level), Base Year 2023 = 100, Monthly: Import Price Index - Overall Items
- evidence: same period; r train +0.28, r test +0.45 (n 101), ex-2020-22 +0.46, BH p 0.004; span 1998-07..2026-09
- explanation: Month-to-month changes in Singapore's 10-year government bond yield tend to move in the same direction as changes in the overall import price index, with no lag (lag 0 months). This was observed over 1998-07 to 2026-09 and held in the later part of the data that was kept aside for testing. It is an association only, not evidence that one drives the other.
- caveat: The relationship is moderate (r_test 0.452), so it explains only part of the month-to-month movement.
- caveat: The two series move in the same month, so neither leads the other and this is not a forecasting signal.
- caveat: The latest data include a very large one-month jump in the import price index (92.69 in 2026-02 to 108.6 in 2026-03), which may carry much of the recent link.
- caveat: Both series may respond to a shared outside factor, such as global commodity prices or inflation expectations, that is not in the data.
- caveat: The yield is an end-of-period figure, while the import price index may be a monthly average or a different measure, so timing within the month may not match exactly.
- idea (verified): Side-by-side tracker of yields and import prices. Plot monthly changes in the 10-year bond yield and the import price index together, highlighting months when they move in the same direction, to show how the same-month association has held up over time.
- idea (verified): Rolling-window stability check. Compute the same-month correlation in rolling multi-year windows from 1998 to 2026 to show when the association was strong or weak, and whether the recent import price jump dominates it.
- idea (brainstormed): Search for a shared driver. Test whether global oil or commodity prices, or regional inflation, explain both the bond yield and import prices, and whether the link disappears once that factor is controlled for.
- reviewer: The 10-year government bond yield and the import price index are different quantities from different agencies, with no identity or component link between them. The link holds out of sample (r_test 0.452 against r_train 0.2807), survives rank correlation (0.4243) and the 2020-2021 exclusion (0.4617), and links financial markets to trade prices in a way a general reader would find mildly surprising.
